The 91899 Derived Grade Survival Kit

The derived grade exam is a written conversation about the digital outcome you built — its purpose, your decisions, the requirements and implications you wrestled with, and what you'd do better next time. This survival kit doesn't show you the exam. It teaches you how to construct and write your answers — from a confident description to a judged evaluation.

Section 1 · Know the terrain

The shape of the exam

Three parts, one journey: describe what you built, connect it to requirements and implications, then judge your own work. This maps the territory — not the questions — so you arrive knowing how to think, not what to copy.

The ground rules · Closed book — everything written from memory. No notes, no internet, no AI. · 800–1,500 words in total across all your answers. · Your own digital outcome from the past 12 months, based on Level 7 of the NZ Curriculum, with at least one developed digital component you created, tested and refined. · No images this year — your words are the only evidence, so every sentence must earn its place. · Know your school code and NSN — you'll type them into the header before you start. · Group project? Write only about your own role.
1

Part 1 — Describe what you made and how

Foundation

The exam opens with the story of your outcome: what you developed and the journey you took to build it.

Write so a stranger could picture your outcome — what it is, why it exists, who it's for, and what it does. Keep it precise, because every later answer will lean on this paragraph.

You'll also describe the development process you followed. This part generally asks you to explain a decision you made along the way — the kind of choice that shaped how the work got done — and to give the reason behind it.

2

Part 2 — Connect to requirements & implications

Where depth is earned

The middle of the exam tests whether your outcome is more than a build — whether it met real-world demands and was shaped by broader considerations.

This part generally asks about a requirement your outcome had to meet — the sort of demand like how it looks, how it works, how usable it is, or how accessible it is — and how you went about addressing it.

It also generally asks about an implication that influenced you while you developed — the bigger-world considerations like people, ethics, the law, ownership of ideas, or the end-users themselves — and the effect it had on what you produced.

3

Part 3 — Evaluate and reflect

Where Excellence lives

The exam finishes by looking back: it generally asks about decisions you made while developing, and it always has some version of "what would you do differently?".

This part generally asks about a couple of decisions you made during development. The expectation is that you judge them, not retell them — weighing whether each worked, how you know, what it cost, and whether you'd make it again.

These exams always close with a "what would you do differently..." style question. Be ready to turn a lesson you learnt during development into a specific improvement to the outcome.

Section 2 · The answer formulas

Formulas for building every answer

Each answer task is a small structure. Fill these frames with your outcome and you're building a response that earns depth — because every frame forces you to add the reason, the evidence or the trade-off that markers reward.

1

Part 1 — Your outcome and the choices you made

Foundation

The opening answers: who, what and why — then the story of how it got built.

"I made a [type of outcome] for [audience], because [purpose]. It contains [main digital components], and its key features are [feature 1, feature 2, feature 3]. I developed the digital component using [software].
I worked through [stage 1] → [stage 2] → [stage 3], and I tested and refined the component by [method].
One important decision I made was to [choice]. I chose this instead of [alternative], because [reason]."
Example answer, broken down

"Decision 1 — I decided to build the navigation and page structure first, before adding any content. I chose this instead of designing each page as I went, because every page depends on the menu — once the structure worked, adding pages was just filling in the blanks.
Decision 2 — I decided to test a paper prototype with a classmate before writing any code. I chose this instead of building the real thing straight away, because a cheap sketch gets feedback fast, and it's much easier to redraw a page than to rewrite finished code."

  • 1 · Decision 1 (sequencing) — navigation/structure built first
  • 2 · The "instead of" — designing each page as I went
  • 3 · The "because" — every page depends on the menu
  • 4 · Decision 2 (end-user considerations) — paper prototype tested with a classmate
  • 5 · The "instead of" — building the real thing straight away
  • 6 · The "because" — a sketch gets feedback fast; easier to redraw than to rewrite

Rule: the "instead of" is the difference between listing a decision and explaining one. Name the road not taken.

2

Part 2 — Requirements, implications and influences

Earns depth

Show that your outcome is more than a build — shaped by real-world demands and wider influences.

"My outcome needed to meet [aesthetics / functionality / usability / accessibility]. To address it, I decided to [action]. What I had to consider was [consideration] — for example, [specific feature or detail].
I was influenced by the [social / ethical / legal / intellectual property / end-user] implication of [issue]. It affected my outcome because [effect]. For example, I [exact change]."
Example answer, broken down

"My website needed to meet the usability requirement, because my audience are older adults who find cluttered pages confusing. To address it, I decided to give every page one clear action. What I had to consider was that too many options overwhelm the reader — for example, I moved the booking form to its own dedicated page. I was also influenced by the social implication of inclusivity — a service should work for everyone, whatever their confidence with technology. It affected my outcome because I chose simple language and large buttons over decorative extras. For example, every button reads as plain text, not an icon, so nothing needs to be guessed."

  • 1 · Name the requirement — usability, linked to the audience
  • 2 · State the decision — one clear action per page
  • 3 · The "what I had to consider" — too many options overwhelm the reader
  • 4 · The specific example — booking form moved to its own page
  • 5 · Name the implication — social / inclusivity
  • 6 · The effect — simple language and large buttons over decorative extras

Rule: an implication only counts if it changed what you built. Your "for example" must be something only your outcome could produce.

3

Part 3 — Judging decisions and what you'd change

Earns Excellence

Judge your own work with evidence, then turn one honest weakness into a specific improvement.

"I decided to [decision] because [reason]. I know it worked because [evidence — a test, a measurement, feedback]. It succeeded in [impact], but it cost me [limitation]. Overall, it was [verdict].
[A test / a user's comment / my own review] showed that [weakness]. I would improve the outcome by [exact change]. This matters to [end-user] because [benefit]."
Example answer, broken down

"I decided to test the site with three real users partway through the build, because my assumptions about how people would use it could be wrong. I know it worked because the testers found two navigation problems I hadn't noticed, and fixing them took minutes while the site was still unfinished. It succeeded in catching problems when they were cheap to fix, but it cost me build time and deadline pressure. Overall, it was the right call.
My second decision was to use SVGs and CSS gradients instead of image files, because the site was loading too slowly. I know it worked because the load time dropped from about six seconds to two. It succeeded in a much lighter site, but it limited the look to clean, flat graphics. Overall, I'd make that trade-off again.
My screen-reader test showed that some buttons were announced as 'link, link', so a blind user couldn't tell what they did. I would improve the outcome by giving every button a clear descriptive label and testing earlier in the build. This matters to screen-reader users because they could navigate the site as easily as anyone else."

  • 1 · Decision 1 — real-user testing partway through the build
  • 2 · The evidence — testers found two navigation problems I'd missed
  • 3 · Success + cost — caught early when cheap to fix, but cost build time
  • 4 · The verdict — right call
  • 5 · Decision 2 — SVGs and CSS gradients instead of image files
  • 6 · The evidence + trade-off — load time 6s → 2s, but a flatter look
  • 7 · The reflection trigger — screen-reader test revealed unclear labels
  • 8 · The change + who it helps — descriptive labels, tested earlier, for screen-reader users

Rule: "I'd do better next time" earns zero. Name the exact change — and the person it helps.

Section 3 · Writing technique

6 techniques that lift every answer

Run every sentence through these rules. If it breaks one, rewrite it before it costs you a grade.

1

Always say why

Every choice needs a reason: "I chose X because Y." No reason, no Merit.

2

Use exact details

Don't say "it works". Say "load time dropped from 6 seconds to 2". If another student could write your sentence, make it yours.

3

Link it all up

Requirement → bigger issue → what you did → real example. If the issue didn't change what you built, don't mention it.

4

Tell the truth about trade-offs

"I chose X over Y because…" Saying what didn't work shows real thinking, not weakness.

5

Judge it, don't retell it

Don't just say what you did. Say if it worked, how you know, and what it cost you.

6

Say who it helps

Every change you'd make should name the person it helps and how. Who is better off because of this?

Section 4 · The grade ladder

What each grade actually means

The levels are cumulative — Merit includes everything at Achieved, and Excellence includes everything at Merit. Climb it: Describe → Explain → Discuss → Evaluate.

Level What the achievement standard requires How this looks in your answers
AchievedA summary of developing the outcome: describing the digital outcome that was developed, and explaining decisions that were made in the development process.A precise picture of your outcome — purpose, audience, components, key features — and your decisions, each with a reason: "I chose X instead of Y, because Z."
MeritEverything at Achieved, plus an in-depth summary: discussing how the digital outcome addressed relevant requirements and implications.Requirements (aesthetics, functionality, usability, accessibility) and implications (social, ethical, legal, intellectual property, end-user considerations) shown to have changed what you actually built — with specific examples.
ExcellenceEverything at Merit, plus a comprehensive summary: evaluating decisions made in the development process and discussing what could be done differently to improve the digital outcome, based on insights gained.Judged decisions with evidence and both sides of the call, honest trade-offs, and an exact improvement grounded in something you learnt — a test, feedback, or a review.
Section 5 · The traps

Fast fixes for the most common fails

Markers see the same mistakes every year. Here's the rewrite that fixes each one.

"My outcome has a homepage and a gallery page."

→

"My outcome lets [user] do [the key thing] quickly and easily."

"The outcome is accessible and usable."

→

"Every button works with only the keyboard — I watched a [user] use it without help."

"Privacy is important, so we thought about it."

→

"Because the app collects [data], I added a consent screen before anything is shared."

"I chose [X] and it made the outcome better."

→

"[A test] showed [result] — but it doubled build time, so polish slipped elsewhere."

"I'd do things differently next time."

→

"[Users] gave up on slow loading, so I'd add [a fix] to keep them."

"It worked great. Everyone loved it. Nothing went wrong."

→

"It worked, except [honest limit] — which is where I found my improvement."

Section 6 · Before you sit the exam

Stock your memory bank

Closed book means your answers come from memory — so before the exam, know these details about your outcome cold. If you can't answer one of these about your own project, that's the gap to close.

Your outcome

The type, its purpose, the target audience, the main digital components, two or three key features, and the software you used to build it.

Your process

The stages in order, what you did at each one, and how you tested and refined the digital component.

One requirement you met

In aesthetics, functionality, usability or accessibility — and the specific feature or detail that proves you met it.

One implication that influenced you

Social, ethical, legal, intellectual property, or end-user considerations — and the exact change it caused to what you built.

Two different decisions

Each one with the alternative you rejected and the "because" — and make sure the second isn't the first restated.

Your evidence

A test, a measurement, or a piece of feedback that shows each decision actually worked.

One honest weakness

Something a test or a user revealed about your outcome, and the exact change you'd make because of it.

The housekeeping

Your school code and NSN, and a rough word budget per part so you don't blow the 800–1,500 total.

How to rehearse

Print the blank paper, put your phone in another room, and write a timed answer from memory. When you finish, check yourself: did I name the alternative I rejected? Did I give evidence for every verdict? Did I say who my improvement helps?
