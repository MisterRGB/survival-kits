The 91909 Derived Grade Survival Kit

The derived grade exam is a written conversation about the digital outcome you developed — its purpose, your decisions, how you learned and grew, and what you'd do better next time. This survival kit doesn't show you the exam. It teaches you how to construct and write your answers — from a confident explanation to an insightful reflective analysis.

Section 1 · Know the terrain

The shape of the exam

Three parts, one journey: explain your outcome, analyse how it came to be, then critique and conclude. This maps the territory — not the questions — so you arrive knowing how to think, not what to copy.

The ground rules · Closed book — everything written from memory. No notes, no internet, no AI. · 800–1,500 words in total across all your answers. · Your own digital outcome from the past 12 months, based on Level 8 of the NZ Curriculum, with at least one substantial digital component you created, tested and refined. · No images this year — your words are the only evidence, so every sentence must earn its place.
1

Part 1 — Your outcome and the choices behind it

Foundation

The exam opens with the story of your outcome — what it is, who it's for, and why it exists — then connects what you made to the decisions behind it.

Write so a stranger could picture your outcome — what it is, why it exists, who it's for, and its main characteristics. Name the software you used and the one substantial component you created, tested and refined, because every later answer will lean on it.

You'll also connect what's visible in your finished outcome back to the decisions that created it. This part generally asks you to link the characteristics of your outcome to the choices behind them — with the reason for each.

2

Part 2 — How your development unfolded

Where depth is earned

The middle of the exam tests whether you can analyse how you developed — your choices, your learning, and the things that shaped you — not just retell what you did.

This part generally asks about the decisions you made while developing — the kind that changed how the work happened — and the effect each had on your process: time, workflow, testing, quality, or the trade-offs it created.

It also generally asks about the things that moved you: new knowledge and skills you gained along the way, feedback from the people your outcome is for, and the bigger-world considerations that shaped what you built and how you built it.

3

Part 3 — Judging your work and drawing conclusions

Where Excellence lives

The exam finishes by looking back: judging what you'd do differently, and drawing conclusions from the experience.

This part generally asks you to judge what you'd do differently to improve both the outcome and how you developed it. The expectation is exact changes grounded in evidence — a test, feedback, a measurement — not vague hopes.

It also generally asks you to draw a conclusion about your development experience: what mattered most, the evidence that proves it, and how that learning would change your approach to a future project.

Section 2 · The answer formulas

Formulas for building every answer

Each answer task is a small structure. Fill these frames with your outcome and you're building a response that earns depth — because every frame forces you to add the reason, the evidence or the trade-off that markers reward.

1

Part 1 — Your outcome and the choices you made

Foundation

The opening answers: who, what and why — then the story of how it got built.

"I made a [type of outcome] for [audience], because [purpose]. Its main characteristics are [characteristic 1, characteristic 2, characteristic 3]. I developed it using [software], and a substantial component was [component], which I created, tested and refined by [method].
One characteristic links to a decision: the [feature] exists because I decided to [choice]. I chose this instead of [alternative], because [reason]."
Example answer, broken down

"I made an interactive 2D game to teach junior students about cyber safety. I chose a game because short levels with instant feedback teach better than a lecture. Its main characteristics are a tutorial with very little text, harder levels as you go, and a live score. I made it in Godot. A substantial component was the coded enemy and scoring system, which I created, tested in different level layouts, and refined so the challenge grew but stayed fair.
The tutorial uses icons and short prompts because I decided to replace long instructions. I chose this instead of a text-heavy tutorial, because playtesting showed players skipped long instructions — the next group finished the first level with much less help."

  • 1 · Name the outcome — type, audience, purpose
  • 2 · The main characteristics — what's visible in the finished product
  • 3 · The substantial component — created, tested and refined
  • 4 · A characteristic linked to a decision — minimal-text tutorial
  • 5 · The "instead of" — a text-heavy tutorial
  • 6 · The "because" — playtesting evidence
2

Part 2 — How your process was shaped

Earns depth

Show that your development was analysed, not just described — tools, feedback, learning and implications all had effects.

"I decided to [decision about tools or techniques] because [reason]. This affected my development process because [effect on time / workflow / testing / quality], and the trade-off was [what it cost].
[Feedback from a stakeholder / an end-user's need] influenced me to [exact change]. This affected the outcome because [effect].
During development I learned [new knowledge or skill]. This guided my next steps because [before → after] — afterwards I could [what became possible].
The [relevant implication — cultural, ethical, legal, privacy, accessibility, usability and so on] influenced my decisions because [effect]. For example, I [exact change]."
Example answer, broken down

"I chose to build the filtering with custom JavaScript, not a template plugin, because I needed more control over how items were sorted. This affected my process because it took more time to debug. I tested the function in small parts and delayed the styling. The trade-off was slow progress early, but a more flexible result in the end.
Stakeholder feedback said my animation moved too quickly, so I decided to extend the key shots and simplify the transitions. The outcome became clearer. But the process took longer, because I had to re-time the audio and redo some cuts.
During development I learned to use parameters and constraints in Fusion 360. This guided my next steps, because I could change measurements without breaking the whole design. After that, I could rebuild parts of the model in minutes instead of starting over.
Privacy affected my decision not to store users' full names on the leaderboard. Instead, I used chosen display names and local storage. This collected less personal data, but progress did not sync across devices and I needed extra testing of the save system."

  • 1 · The tools decision + the "because" — custom JS, more control
  • 2 · The effect on the process + trade-off — time, testing, styling
  • 3 · The stakeholder influence + the change — feedback that moved you
  • 4 · The effect on outcome AND process — clearer result, reworked audio
  • 5 · The new knowledge + before/after — parameters and constraints
  • 6 · The implication + the exact change — privacy → display names
3

Part 3 — Critique, improve and conclude

Earns Excellence

Judge your work with evidence, turn honest weaknesses into specific improvements — and take a lesson forward.

"I would improve the outcome by [exact change], because [evidence — a test, a measurement, feedback] showed that [weakness]. This matters to [end-user] because [benefit].
I would improve the process by [exact change], because [what went wrong in the process] meant [consequence].
The most significant factor in my outcome's success was [factor], proven by [evidence]. This taught me [learning], so in a future project I would [approach], because [reason]."
Example answer, broken down

"I would improve the outcome by adding a sticky mobile menu. Testing showed users often scrolled back to the top to change sections. This matters to mobile users, because the menu would always be visible and they could check information quickly on a phone.
I would improve the process by testing an interactive prototype earlier. I left stakeholder testing until the outcome was almost finished, so navigation problems were found late and were harder to fix.
The most significant factor in my success was early user testing. In the first test, most players skipped the text-heavy tutorial, so I replaced it with icons and short prompts. The next group finished the first level with much less help. This taught me that a complex feature is only useful if users can understand it. In a future project, I would test with users after every major feature."

  • 1 · The outcome improvement + the evidence — sticky menu, testing showed
  • 2 · The exact fix + who it helps — mobile users
  • 3 · The process improvement + why it's needed — late testing, hard fixes
  • 4 · The most significant factor + the evidence — early user testing
  • 5 · The learning + the future approach — test after every major feature
Section 3 · Writing technique

6 techniques that lift every answer

Run every sentence through these rules. If it breaks one, rewrite it before it costs you a grade.

1

Always say why

Every choice needs a reason: "I chose X instead of Y, because Z." No reason, no Merit.

2

Use exact details

Don't say "it works". Say "completion rose from 40% to 85%". If another student could write your sentence, make it yours.

3

Analyse the process, not just the product

Every decision should show its effect on how you worked. If a choice changed nothing, it earns nothing.

4

Tell the truth about trade-offs

"I chose X over Y because…" Saying what it cost shows real thinking, not weakness.

5

Show your learning changed you

New knowledge only counts with a before and after. What could you do afterwards that you couldn't before?

6

Judge it, don't retell it

Don't just say what you did. Say if it worked, how you know, what it cost — then draw the conclusion.

Section 4 · The grade ladder

What each grade actually means

The levels are cumulative — Merit includes everything at Achieved, and Excellence includes everything at Merit. Climb it: Explain → Link → Analyse → Critique → Conclude.

Level What the achievement standard requires How this looks in your answers
AchievedPresent a reflective analysis of developing a digital outcome, which involves: explaining the digital outcome that was created, and linking the outcome's characteristics to decisions that were made in the development process.A precise picture of your outcome — purpose, audience, main characteristics, your substantial component — plus characteristics traced back to decisions, each with a reason: "the [feature] exists because I decided to [choice] instead of [alternative]."
MeritEverything at Achieved, plus present an in-depth reflective analysis of developing a digital outcome, which involves: analysing how decisions made affected the development process, and explaining how new knowledge and skills guided the development process.Every decision analysed for its effect on time, workflow, testing or quality — with trade-offs — and new knowledge shown with a before/after that changed your next steps.
ExcellenceEverything at Merit, plus present an insightful reflective analysis of developing a digital outcome, which involves: critiquing what could be done differently to improve both the outcome and the development process, and drawing conclusions about the outcome and/or development process.Judged improvements to the outcome AND the process, each grounded in evidence, plus a conclusion that names the most significant factor and how your learning changes your future approach.
Section 5 · The traps

Fast fixes for the most common fails

Markers see the same mistakes every year. Here's the rewrite that fixes each one.

"I made a game in Godot."

→

"My outcome was an interactive 2D game for [audience], with a substantial [component] I created, tested and refined."

"People said it was good."

→

"Playtesters found the difficulty spike too high, so I adjusted enemy speed — completion rose from 40% to 85%."

"I learned to use Godot Resources."

→

"Discovering [skill] was the missing piece — it let me build [system], which unlocked the whole game loop."

"I chose custom JavaScript and it made the outcome better."

→

"[The choice] gave me more control, but it increased debugging time — so I tested in smaller parts and delayed the styling."

"Next time I'd manage my time better."

→

"Testing showed users scrolled back to the top to change sections, so I'd add a sticky mobile menu."

"My outcome was successful."

→

"The most significant factor was [factor], proven by [evidence] — so in future I'd [approach]."

Section 6 · Before you sit the exam

Stock your memory bank

Closed book means your answers come from memory — so before the exam, know these details about your outcome cold. If you can't answer one of these about your own project, that's the gap to close.

Your outcome

The type, purpose, audience, main characteristics, the software you used, and your one substantial component that was created, tested and refined.

Characteristics linked to decisions

Each with the "instead of" and the "because" — and make sure every characteristic is visible in the finished outcome.

One decision about tools or techniques

And its effect on your process: time, workflow, testing, quality — plus the trade-off it created.

One stakeholder or end-user influence

Who they were, what they said or needed, the change you made because of it, and what it cost.

New knowledge and skills

What you learned during development and the before/after that shows it guided your next steps.

One relevant implication

And the exact change it caused to what you built or how you built it — a detail only your outcome could produce.

One outcome improvement

The weakness, the evidence that shows it, the exact fix, and the person it helps.

One process improvement

What went wrong in how you worked — not the product — and the exact fix for next time.

Your conclusion

The most significant factor in your outcome's success, the evidence that proves it, and how that learning changes your future approach.

The housekeeping

Your school code and NSN, your outcome type and main software, and a rough word budget per part so you don't blow the 800–1,500 total. If the exam groups two answers into one response space, keep them together and cover both.

How to rehearse

Print the blank paper, put your phone in another room, and write a timed answer from memory. When you finish, check yourself: did I name the alternative I rejected? Did I give evidence for every verdict? Did I show a before/after for my new knowledge? Did I say who my improvements help?
